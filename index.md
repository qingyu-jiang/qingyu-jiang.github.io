<h1 class="visually-hidden">About Qingyu Jiang</h1>
<div class="profile">
  {% include academic-portrait.html %}
  <div class="biography" markdown="1">

Qingyu Jiang is an Assistant Professor of Psychology in the Department of Psychology and Communication at the University of Idaho, where she directs the [Well-being, Health, Early Adaptation and Thriving (WHEAT) Lab]({{ "/wheat-lab/" | relative_url }}). She is also an affiliate faculty member with the Margaret Ritchie School of Family and Consumer Sciences and the Center of Biomedical Research Excellence in Nutrition and Women’s Health. She received her Ph.D. in Human Sciences, with a specialization in Child, Youth, and Family Studies, from the University of Nebraska-Lincoln. She then completed a postdoctoral fellowship in maternal and child mental health at Brigham and Women’s Hospital/Harvard Medical School.

  </div>
</div>

<div class="home-narrative" markdown="1">

Dr. Jiang’s research focuses on how developmental contexts, experiences, and relationships shape mental health and child development. Recognizing that the early years lay the foundation for lifelong health and well-being, she studies families from pregnancy through infancy and early childhood, tracing how early experiences relate to development throughout childhood and adolescence. Her research connects three complementary areas: 1) how family, community, social, and cultural contexts shape children’s development and adaptation; 2) how caregiver well-being relates to parenting and early childhood development; and 3) how parents’ use of generative artificial intelligence (AI) shapes mental health, parenting, and family relationships.

She approaches these questions by recognizing families’ strengths alongside the challenges they face and examining how social and economic conditions shape their opportunities for health and well-being. Her methods include statistical analyses of data collected over time, qualitative inquiry into families’ experiences, and mixed methods research that brings these approaches together. Across this work, she identifies opportunities within environments and relationships to strengthen resilience and support healthy development.

She conducts this research with particular attention to racially and ethnically diverse families, families experiencing economic hardship, and families facing barriers to accessing and using emerging technologies for information and support. Her goal is to use this evidence to inform practical prevention and intervention strategies that respond to families’ circumstances and strengthen support for underserved children and families.
